Your Mind is a Field, Not a Film: 4 Revelations from a New Theory of Thought

Most of us experience our own thinking as a private movie theater. We have a "mind's eye" that plays back memories or visualizes future possibilities, and an "inner voice" that narrates our day, rehearses conversations, or reads these very words. These internal experiences are so fundamental that we often equate them with the act of thinking itself.

But what if this internal world of images and sounds is not thought, but just an optional user interface for a much deeper, more abstract cognitive process? What if pure cognition doesn't need pictures or words at all? A mind-bending new framework from theoretical physics and cognitive science, called the "Relativistic Scalar–Vector Plenum" (RSVP), formalizes this radical idea. It proposes that cognition is a physical process where the mind-body-world system seeks stability, and that the fundamental nature of thought is a "direct traversal of semantic geometry"—a process that happens in a silent, imageless space of meaning.

This is a complex, mathematically dense theory, but its core theme is transformative: thinking is a physical process of finding equilibrium. Here are the four most impactful takeaways, distilled into plain English.

1. Your Inner World is an Optional 'Display Screen'

The first major revelation of the RSVP framework is that mental images and inner speech are "optional projections," not the fundamental process of thought. The theory posits that core thinking—what it calls semantic computation—happens on a foundational layer, or "base manifold." This core process involves a field of "semantic intensity" (Φ) that ebbs and flows, representing the landscape of meaning.

What we experience as a visual image or a snippet of inner speech are just projections from this deeper field onto a visual layer (πV) or an auditory layer (πA). You can think of the base manifold as your computer's core processor, crunching data and running algorithms. Your mind's eye and inner voice are like the monitor and speakers—useful outputs, but not the computation itself. According to Cox's Mimetic Hypothesis, a key part of this theory, the mind can use the body's motor systems as a scaffold for thought even without a sensory display. Aphantasia (the inability to form mental images) is like running the computer "headless." The processing is still happening perfectly; it's just not being rendered on that particular screen.

Within RSVP, imagery and inner speech are not constitutive of thought; they are optional projections that some agents forgo without functional loss.

This idea is powerful because it cleanly separates the act of thinking from the act of experiencing those thoughts in a sensory format. The underlying cognitive engine can run silently and invisibly, yet still perform reasoning, planning, and creative tasks with complete competence.

2. 'Imageless Thought' Isn't a Bug, It's a Different Setting

For decades, conditions like aphantasia (no mind's eye) and anendophasia (no inner voice) have been studied, often with an implicit assumption of deficit. The RSVP model completely reframes this, suggesting these are not bugs, but simply different, entirely valid operational modes of cognition. One reason this form of thought isn't a deficit is because cognition can be channeled through "covert motor simulation"—an idea formalized by the Mimetic Hypothesis.

The theory introduces a mechanism called "CLIO" (Cognitive Loop via In-Situ Optimization), an adaptive control system that tunes the brain's parameters. One key parameter is the "CLIO gain" (κCLIO), which can be thought of as a cognitive dial controlling the intensity of sensory projections. When the gain is turned up high, the signal for sensory modes can cross a threshold, resulting in vivid, conscious imagery. When the gain is lower, or "subcritical," it "sustains the silent base" of pure, imageless thought.

Crucially, this isn't just a metaphor. The theory makes a specific, falsifiable prediction: the CLIO gain parameter should negatively correlate with scores on the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ), with a predicted correlation of r < -0.5. This grounds the abstract model in a concrete, testable hypothesis and implies that neurodiversity isn't just about differences in brain wiring, but potentially about differences in these fundamental operational settings.

3. Your Notepad Is Literally Part of Your Mind

The RSVP framework provides a formal, mathematical basis for the "extended mind"—the idea that our cognitive processes can literally extend into our environment. It does this through a concept called "Embodied Stigmergy" (stigmergy being a mechanism of indirect coordination, like when ants use pheromone trails), which models how we use external artifacts to think.

The model uses coupled partial differential equations to describe how our internal cognitive field (Φint) interacts with an external field (Φext), such as the ink on a piece of paper. When you sketch a diagram to solve a problem, you are not just offloading memory. According to RSVP, you are engaging in an active feedback loop between your brain and the paper. The marks on the page become a literal extension of your cognitive state, creating persistent traces that stabilize informational disorder (S) and drive the thought process forward.

This concept also comes with a testable prediction. The theory predicts that using external aids can act as a cognitive equalizer, with diagramming expected to reduce group differences in task performance to a negligible level (effect size d < 0.1) between people with and without aphantasia. This implies that the boundary between our minds and our tools is not just blurry, but a mathematically coupled system.

4. Thinking is a Physical Process of 'Smoothing Out' Your Thoughts

Perhaps the most surprising takeaway is that the process of thinking can be described using the language of physics. The theory includes an "H-Theorem Analog," a principle stating that the total "configurational complexity" or "informational disorder" of the cognitive system naturally tends to decrease (d/dt ∫ S dV ≤ 0).

In accessible terms, this means that thinking is a process that spontaneously moves from a state of higher complexity and disorder toward one of greater order, stability, and clarity. Imagine a wrinkled sheet that, over time, naturally smooths itself out. The theory posits that cognition works in a similar way. The struggle with a complex, confusing problem corresponds to a high-complexity, high-disorder cognitive state. The moment of clarity—the "aha!"—is the cognitive field finally settling into a stable, low-complexity configuration.

This connects a deep physical principle to a profoundly familiar subjective experience. The feeling of "gaining clarity" isn't just a metaphor; it's a reflection of a real, physical process of complexity descent occurring within the field of your mind, bringing the entire mind-body-world system closer to equilibrium.

A New Vision of the Mind

Together, these takeaways paint a new picture of the mind, shifting our view from a world of internal pictures and words to a dynamic, physical field of meaning that seeks equilibrium with our bodies and the world. This field can operate silently and invisibly, or it can project its activity onto our familiar sensory "screens." It's a vision of cognition that is more fundamental, more flexible, and more deeply integrated with the physical world than we ever imagined.

The RSVP framework suggests our mind is not a computer running software, but a physical field seeking equilibrium with its environment. If a sketchpad can literally become part of our thought process, what does that imply for our relationship with increasingly intelligent tools, and where, precisely, do 'we' end and our creations begin?
