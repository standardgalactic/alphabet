##  
Effective Assembly Index for Mind Recognition (EAIMR)
 
Definition
The Effective Assembly Index for Mind Recognition is a measure of the improbability of a structure, behavior, or system arising from unguided physical processes, adjusted for the presence of reuse, parallelism, degeneracy, and coherence. A high EAIMR suggests the presence of a mind-like process coordinating the assembly.
##  
Formal Structure
 
\text{EAIMR}(X) ;=; \frac{A(X)}{R(X) \cdot P(X) \cdot D(X)} \cdot C(X)
 
Where:
 
 = Raw Assembly Index of structure/system
(minimum steps required to build from primitives).
 
 = Reuse factor: the extent to which pre-assembled modules (motifs, subroutines, ecological niches) are leveraged. Higher reuse lowers effective difficulty.
 
 = Parallelism factor: the degree to which independent assembly steps can occur simultaneously. Higher parallelism lowers effective difficulty.
 
 = Degeneracy factor: the number of functionally equivalent alternatives to . High degeneracy spreads probability mass, lowering difficulty.
 
 = Coherence factor: a multiplier reflecting functional integration, adaptivity, or goal-directedness of the system. Higher coherence boosts recognition of mind-like properties.
##  
Interpretation
 
High EAIMR → unlikely to be explained by blind physics; suggests cognitive, agentic, or mind-like processes.
 
Low EAIMR → plausibly explained by physical self-organization or stochastic assembly without mind-like agency.

## Definition and Origin
The
**praxicon**
in cognitive science refers to a structured set of long-term memory representations for familiar motor actions—essentially, a "dictionary" of gesture and action concepts. It originated from neuropsychological models of limb praxis, notably by Rothi, Heilman, and colleagues, distinguishing input praxicon (recognition, visuo-gestural) and output praxicon (production, motor implementation).[2][4][5][1]
## Functional Role
**Input praxicon**
encodes perceptual and visuo-gestural attributes of familiar actions for recognition and discrimination.[2]
**Output praxicon**
contains the motor engrams or procedural encodings needed for gesture and tool-use execution.[2]
Both praxicons interface with an action-semantic system, mediating between abstract concepts and specific action routines.[3][2]
This architecture enables mapping between language, perception, and motor control—a bridge between symbolic and embodied cognition.[1][3]
## Research Context and Applications
Recent computational and robotic work has expanded the praxicon to resources that semantically ground language in motoric/action primitives, supporting multimodal AI and cognitive robotics.[3][1]
Neuroimaging studies link the praxicon's neural substrates to parietal-premotor circuits, especially in praxis and apraxia research.[4][2]
The concept underpins research in gesture recognition, tool-use disorders, and the representation of actions in memory and language.[5][6][3]

The assembly index (as used in origin-of-life research and complexity science) measures the minimum number of steps required to build a given structure from its basic components. It's meant to distinguish things that arise easily from physics/chemistry (low assembly index) versus things that suggest directed processes (high assembly index, e.g. proteins, machines).
 
But there may indeed be loopholes in how it's applied:
 
Reuse of substructures: If a system has access to pre-assembled modules (e.g. repeating motifs in polymers, or standardized software libraries), the effective assembly index collapses. Complexity can be bootstrapped without "paying the full cost."
 
Parallelism: The index assumes a linear build sequence, but many natural processes are massively parallel. Parallel pathways can drastically shorten the real "time to assembly."
 
Context dependence: Some structures look astronomically unlikely in isolation, but in a rich environment (e.g. Earth's oceans, or the digital commons) precursors are already abundant. This reduces effective assembly difficulty.
 
Degeneracy: Many distinct structures can fulfill the same function. The assembly index of one particular molecule might be huge, but life doesn't need that exact one — just any of a vast family. So probability mass spreads out.
 
Information leakage: If external agents (humans, AI, evolutionary processes) leave behind "scaffolds," then future builders inherit shortcuts. The index doesn't always account for cumulative culture.

Aphantasia (lack of mental imagery) and anendophasia (lack of inner monologue) are not usually framed as disabilities. Yet in an economy that rewards constant self-promotion, visualization, and storytelling, they place people at a systemic disadvantage.
 
The attention economy assumes every mind is equally capable of projecting compelling narratives about the self. When those narratives become the ticket to employment, status, or belonging, individuals without the culturally privileged forms of imagination and narration are quietly excluded.
 
This is an intersubjectivity fault line: as society increasingly equates worth with performance in digital arenas, those whose cognition doesn't translate smoothly into attention-seeking formats risk being rendered invisible.
 
 
Just as roads fragment ecosystems and feeds fragment meaning, the attention economy fragments humanity by creating cognitive castes. Those who can continuously narrate and promote thrive; those who can't are marginalized. This is another vector by which intersubjectivity collapse advances: shared meaning narrows to the language of promotion, leaving out entire ways of being human.
