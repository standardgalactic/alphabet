1. **Nonfluent Aphasia**: 
   - Characterized by effortful, telegraphic speech lacking grammatical morphemes like function words (prepositions, pronouns) and inflectional morphemes (tense).
   - Examples include Broca’s aphasia where production of language is impaired.
   
2. **Fluent Aphasia**:
   - Speech remains fluent but lacks meaning or contains incorrect word choices. 
   - Wernicke's aphasia is a type of fluent aphasia marked by poor comprehension and nonsensical speech.

3. **Specific Syndromes**:
   - **Agrammatism**: Impairment in grammar both productionally (speech) and receptively (comprehension), though recent studies suggest that syntactic knowledge isn't lost but may be processed more slowly.
   - **Paragrammatism**: Preserves grammatical structure but involves errors in lexical or functional elements, typically linked to posterior temporal damage.

1. **Brain Damage Impact**:
   - Left hemisphere injuries often affect language processing, with frontal lesions associated with agrammatism (impaired grammar) and posterior/temporal damage linked to paragrammatism.
   
2. **Language Network Dynamics**:
   - The brain's response to injury involves phases: acute, subacute, and chronic.
   - Reorganization may include increased activity in perilesional cortex (areas near the lesion), recruitment of homologous regions in the opposite hemisphere, or engagement of domain-general networks.

3. **Plasticity and Compensation**:
   - The brain employs multiple strategies to recover language functions: restoration within spared areas and compensation via broader cognitive systems.
   - Real-time compensatory mechanisms are evident where semantic processing adjusts for deficits in grammatical processing.
