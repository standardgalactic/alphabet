De Arca Noë: Dialogus Asinorum
(A Dialogue on Noah's Ark, attributed to Giordano Bruno)
Persons:
Noah, the patriarch
Naama, his wife
Lorenzo, a llama, interpreter of beasts
Several Monks, listeners and scoffers
The Fox, sly counsellor
The Donkey, laborious and indignant
Scene: A monastery courtyard, Florence, 1570. A circle of friars gathers around Brother Giordano, who speaks half in jest, half in earnest.
Bruno (narrating):
On the Ark of Noah, where beasts of every shape were packed tighter than peas in a pod, there arose complaints most ridiculous and most profound. The donkeys, sweating under bundles of hay, groaned at their place in the dark hold. "Why must lions bask near the windows while we choke in straw and dung?" they cried.
Fox (smiling):
"My long-eared friends, is it not written that the humble shall serve, while the noble shall shine? Your braying is the very music that steadies the ship!"
Donkey (stamping hoof):
"Our labor is constant, your counsel empty. Why should cunning sit above toil?"
Lorenzo the Llama (stepping forth):
"Peace, good creatures! I, Lorenzo, who chew cud with patience, shall bear your words to Noah himself. For I speak both beast and man, though some call me a gossip."
(The friars laugh; one mutters, "Truly, Giordano invents animals with longer tongues than himself!")
Noah (hearing the dispute):
"Lorenzo, what troubles the ark today? Already I must mend leaks and calm storms — must I also calm quarrels of stable and sty?"
Lorenzo:
"Father Noah, the donkeys complain of their low estate. The fox justifies it as the natural order. Yet resentment breeds like mildew in damp straw. If this ark carries not only flesh but the seed of future nations, should not justice also sail with us?"
Noah (to Naama):
"Wife, fetch the tablets. If men require laws, why not beasts as well? Let us set down precepts to guide even their braying."
Naama (teaching the letters):
With a stylus she scratched upon planks seven simple marks, and — miracle of miracles — the animals bent their necks to learn. Thus she spoke:
Harmony — Each shall respect the place of the other.
Labor — Each shall carry according to its strength.
Measure — None shall hoard beyond its need.
Counsel — Even the smallest voice may speak in council.
Compassion — The strong shall shield the weak.
Memory — What is suffered shall not be forgotten.
Hope — All shall look toward the new land with equal eyes.
Fox (aside, grumbling):
"A fine sermon! Yet laws are nets, and I know well how to slip through nets..."
Donkey (braying loudly):
"At last! We too have a voice, no longer beasts of burden only, but beasts of reason!"
Bruno (turning to the monks):
And so, brethren, the Ark became not merely a floating barn, but the first republic of beasts. The fox still schemed, the donkeys still groaned, and Lorenzo still carried tales — but all knew there was a law higher than tooth or hoof.
Now tell me, brothers: was this only a fable of animals, or is it not rather the world itself? For are we not all passengers, beasts and men alike, tossed on the flood of time, ruled by foxes, burdened as donkeys, needing a law not from princes but from the justice of nature?
(The monks shift uneasily. One laughs, another frowns. Is it safe to laugh at foxes and friars in the same breath?)
Bruno, with a sly smile:
Laugh if you wish, but remember: the Ark lands not only on Ararat, but in every age where beasts demand their dignity.
That's the fragment. It begins with burlesque detail (carrots, dung, Lorenzo's gossip), shifts into Noachide laws by Naama, and ends with Bruno's unmistakable turn from jest to dangerous allegory: the Ark = the Church, foxes = the clergy, donkeys = the laity.
