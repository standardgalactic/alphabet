Roads Not Taken: Encoding Ratchets in Cinema, Computation, and Cosmology
Introduction
Histories of media, machines, and models often appear linear: cinema progresses from reels to digital projection, computation from teletypes to neural networks, cosmology from Einstein to ΛCDM. Yet these trajectories reflect not inevitability but encoding ratchets—arbitrary representational choices that, once standardized, scale efficiently and foreclose alternatives.
Scaling, as Richard Sutton's Bitter Lesson (2019) reminds us, is powerful. But scaling multiplies whichever encodings happened to lock in early, not necessarily those best suited to capture reality. This essay explores three cases—cinema, computation, and cosmology—to argue that scaling can conceal deeper alternatives. Recent work by Giani, von Marttens, and Camilleri (2025) on effective-fluid cosmology highlights how close mainstream physics is now coming to the entropic and structural intuitions formalized in the Relativistic Scalar Vector Plenum (RSVP).
Cinema's Encoding Ratchet
1.1 The Frame Standard
Cinema ratcheted into 2D frames on reels, chosen for ease of duplication and projection. This encoding made cinema a global monoculture: one film, one set of actors, identical everywhere.
1.2 Suppressed Alternatives
Volumetric cinema: early stereoscopic systems suggested films could have been encoded as three-dimensional scenes, allowing each theater to re-project slightly different perspectives.
Local actor substitution: in the 1930s, parallel-language versions of films were re-shot with local casts. Had this scaled, cinema might resemble distributed theater: global plots, local embodiments.
Frames scaled best, so they won.
Computation's Encoding Ratchet
2.1 ASCII and the Keyboard
Computing ratcheted into ASCII and keyboards, initially for military and telecommunication compatibility. This encoded machines as linear text processors, a lineage inherited today by tokenized AI models.
2.2 Suppressed Alternatives
Cursive machines: IBM handwriting recognizers (1950s) and Apple's Newton (1990s) show computing could have scaled around cursive input and pen output.
Flashcard machines: Vannevar Bush's Memex and B. F. Skinner's teaching devices envisioned modular, card-based information storage and recall—computation as memory augmentation, not stream manipulation.
ASCII scaled better, so it won.
Cosmology's Encoding Ratchet
3.1 The ΛCDM Paradigm
Modern cosmology ratcheted into the ΛCDM model: a homogeneous background with perturbations. Non-linear structures were treated as negligible, their backreaction assumed to vanish. This choice fit perturbation theory and computational scaling.
Sidebar 1 (Backreaction in plain terms):
Imagine stirring a pot of thick soup. Some regions clump into denser lumps; other regions thin out into bubbles. If you treat the soup as perfectly uniform, you will mis-estimate how it heats and circulates. "Backreaction" is simply the recognition that lumps and voids feed back into the global behavior of the whole pot.
3.2 Suppressed Alternatives
Backreaction models: Buchert averaging and relativistic simulations showed hints that collapsing clusters and expanding voids might matter for the global expansion, but these approaches never scaled computationally.
Effective fluids: Giani, von Marttens, and Camilleri (2025) recently modeled collapsing and expanding regions as two effective fluids with non-zero equations of state. This representation, fit to DESI and supernova data, eases the Hubble and  tensions—longstanding problems of ΛCDM.
RSVP cosmology: RSVP represents collapse and voids not as phenomenological fluids but as intrinsic scalar-vector-entropy field dynamics. What the effective-fluid model encodes phenomenologically, RSVP derives as microphysics: compressive modes (), expansive modes (), and their couplings (lamphron-lamphrodyne transfers).
3.3 A Convergence of Intuitions
The surprise is that mainstream cosmology is now arriving, via effective fluids, at insights RSVP predicted:
The apparent weakening of dark energy can be reinterpreted as the entropic footprint of structure formation.¹
The resolution of the Hubble tension emerges not from new fields but from recognizing collapsing and expanding regions as active components.²
The σ8 tension is likewise eased once effective collapse and void terms modify growth.
Here cosmology mirrors cinema and computation: a contingent encoding (smooth FLRW + perturbations) scaled best, but alternative encodings (backreaction fluids, RSVP fields) may prove richer.
Note 1 (Dark energy "weakening"):
In ΛCDM, dark energy is assumed constant. But if you ignore the effect of voids and clusters, the data looks like dark energy is changing. Giani's model shows that what appears to be "weakening energy" is really just the uneven texture of the universe being misread. RSVP makes a similar claim: the cosmos does not need literal expansion—apparent acceleration is the echo of entropic redistribution.
Note 2 (Hubble tension in plain terms):
The Hubble tension is the mismatch between two rulers: the early-universe ruler (CMB) and the late-universe ruler (supernovae, BAO). If you account for voids and collapsing regions, you are not stretching the ruler, just admitting that it is dented and bent in places. The mismatch shrinks.
Scaling and the Bitter Lesson
The Bitter Lesson tells us that general methods plus scaling outperform clever designs. Yet history shows scaling amplifies whatever encoding was already ratcheted in:
ASCII scaled better than cursive.
Frames scaled better than volumetric cinema.
ΛCDM scaled better than backreaction models.
This does not mean those encodings are uniquely right—only that they were the most scalable at the time.
RSVP as a Counter-Ratchet
RSVP resists ratchets by keeping multiple encodings alive:
In cognition, it encodes processes as scalar-vector-entropy field flows, not tokens.
In cosmology, it encodes backreaction as entropic dynamics, not as dark energy or effective fluids.
In philosophy of science, it suggests that suppressed encodings (local actors, flashcards, collapsing voids) can coexist as concurrent computational modes.
Where Sutton emphasizes the inevitability of scaling, RSVP emphasizes the choice of representation: scaling multiplies, but the quality of what it multiplies depends on whether we locked in the right encoding.
Conclusion
Cinema, computation, and cosmology all illustrate the same lesson: what scaled is not necessarily what was most true, only what was most compatible with infrastructure at the time. Recent effective-fluid models of collapse and voids show that even cosmology is rediscovering the power of alternative encodings. RSVP goes further, embedding those encodings in a unified field-theoretic plenum.
The roads not taken in cinema and computation produced monocultures; the road not taken in cosmology may have obscured the real drivers of the Universe's apparent acceleration. RSVP stands as a reminder that suppressed paths can be reopened—and that the future of intelligence and cosmology alike depends not only on scaling, but on choosing the right encodings before we let scaling run away.

A.2 Ratchets in Computational Architecture
The essay emphasized ASCII and keyboards as ratchets of representation. But an equally consequential ratchet occurred in computer architecture: the adoption of the von Neumann model in the 1940s. This architecture—single memory for code and data, sequential instruction execution—scaled brilliantly with early hardware, but it was not inevitable.
A.2.1 The von Neumann Ratchet
Advantages: Simplicity of design, compatibility with symbolic programming, easy scaling of transistor counts.
Consequences: Bottlenecks in memory bandwidth ("von Neumann bottleneck"), strong orientation toward sequential symbolic processing, and reinforcement of the ASCII/text-stream paradigm.
A.2.2 Suppressed Alternatives
Dataflow architectures
Programs represented as graphs of data dependencies.
Computation proceeds when inputs are ready, inherently parallel.
Early experiments (MIT Tagged Token architecture, 1970s) showed promise but could not scale cheaply with hardware of the time.
Ratcheted out by the dominance of sequential instruction sets.
Neural and analog computing
Mechanical/electronic analog machines in the 1930s-1950s solved differential equations directly.
Neuromorphic concepts (McCulloch-Pitts networks, later Carver Mead's silicon neurons) offered parallelism and efficiency.
Lost momentum in the transistor era when digital sequential logic scaled faster.
Associative / content-addressable memory
Instead of fetching data by address, retrieval is pattern-based ("give me the row that matches this key").
Would have supported memory-intensive AI tasks naturally.
Remained niche (in networking hardware, caches) while mainstream computing entrenched address-based access.
Stack and functional machines
Burroughs B5000 (1961) embodied a stack machine with direct support for high-level languages.
Functional programming languages (LISP, FP) could have ratcheted differently if stack machines had scaled.
Instead, C and von Neumann-style imperative languages dominated, reinforcing text-stream ASCII encodings.
A.2.3 The RSVP Counterfactual
From the RSVP perspective, these suppressed architectures resemble roads not taken in cosmology:
von Neumann = ΛCDM perturbation theory (efficient scaling, but rigid encoding).
Dataflow = backreaction/field encoding (explicit non-linearity, natural parallelism).
Analog/neuromorphic = RSVP plenum simulation itself (continuous fields, entropic flows).
Associative memory = TARTAN/RSVP semantic tilings (pattern-based access, trajectory memory).
Had dataflow or neuromorphic systems scaled first, the culture of computation might have favored RSVP-style field encodings from the beginning rather than ASCII streams.
A.3 Ratchets and Polycomputational Futures
The lesson from both cosmology and computation is that ratchets lock in encodings that scale best with available hardware, even if they are not the most expressive.
In cosmology, ΛCDM suppressed non-linear encodings; effective-fluid models and RSVP reopen them.
In computation, von Neumann suppressed dataflow, associative, and analog architectures; RSVP-inspired systems could revive them for a polycomputational future.
The danger, as Sutton's Bitter Lesson shows, is mistaking what scaled for what was inevitable. The task now is to resist single ratchets by designing architectures—scientific and computational—that preserve multiplicity of encodings. RSVP is one such attempt: a plenum in which collapse and expansion, token and field, discrete and continuous, can all scale together.

A.2 Ratchets in Computational Architecture
The adoption of the von Neumann architecture—a sequential model where a single memory stores both instructions and data—was not inevitable. It scaled well with vacuum tubes and later transistors, but other architectures existed that might have locked in had conditions been slightly different.
A.2.1 Dataflow Machines
Concept: Programs represented as dependency graphs. Computation proceeds when inputs are ready; instructions fire asynchronously.
Examples: MIT Tagged Token Dataflow Machine (1970s), Manchester Dataflow Computer (1980s).
Promise: Natural parallelism; eliminates the von Neumann bottleneck.
Failure to scale: Hardware complexity and lack of compiler support made them slower and more expensive than von Neumann machines in the short term.
Counterfactual: If dataflow had scaled, programming would look more like wiring RSVP's field equations directly—flows, dependencies, and event-driven computation—rather than issuing sequential instructions.
A.2.2 Analog and Neuromorphic Computing
Analog machines: Vannevar Bush's Differential Analyzer (1930s) solved differential equations with gears, shafts, and wheels.
Neuromorphic chips: Carver Mead (1980s) pioneered silicon neurons and synapses, aiming for brainlike computation.
Promise: Direct representation of continuous dynamics; energy-efficient and massively parallel.
Failure to scale: Digital logic was easier to miniaturize and manufacture reliably.
Counterfactual: An analog/neuromorphic ratchet would have normalized RSVP-style continuous field dynamics, making scalar-vector-entropy simulation the default mode of computing.
A.2.3 Associative and Content-Addressable Memory (CAM)
Concept: Retrieve data by its content, not by an address. Ask the memory, "Who matches this pattern?" rather than "What is stored at address X?"
Examples: Early associative memories in the 1960s; used in networking and caches but never mainstream.
Promise: Pattern recognition and search become fundamental operations—ideal for AI.
Failure to scale: Too costly in silicon area; industry standardized on address-based RAM.
Counterfactual: An associative-memory ratchet would have fit perfectly with RSVP's TARTAN framework: computation as semantic tiling and trajectory memory, not instruction-address cycles.
A.2.4 Stack and Functional Machines
Burroughs B5000 (1961): A pioneering stack machine designed to run ALGOL directly. Programs executed as high-level blocks; hardware understood recursion.
Promise: Close alignment between human reasoning (functions, recursion) and machine execution.
Failure to scale: Less efficient for low-level tasks, overtaken by IBM System/360 and C-based architectures.
Counterfactual: If the B5000 lineage had won, programming culture might have ratcheted toward functional abstraction and symbolic manipulation, not imperative token streams. RSVP's categorical formulations would feel less alien in such an ecosystem.
A.3 RSVP and Architectural Counterfactuals
Seen through RSVP, each alternative architecture corresponds to a "road not taken" in computation, much like effective-fluid cosmology represents a road not taken in physics:
Von Neumann ↔ ΛCDM perturbation theory: Scales well, but encodes reality narrowly.
Dataflow ↔ backreaction averaging: Emphasizes flows and dependencies, natural parallelism, but too costly early on.
Analog/neuromorphic ↔ RSVP plenum: Continuous fields, entropic flows, microphysical realism; suppressed by digital ratchet.
Associative memory ↔ TARTAN/semantic tilings: Pattern-based retrieval and modular computation; eclipsed by address-based memory.
Stack machines ↔ categorical/sheaf encodings: Function-first design, structurally aligned with higher mathematics; suppressed by imperative hardware/software co-evolution.
A.4 Polycomputational Futures
The common lesson across cinema, computation, and cosmology is that ratchets lock in whichever encoding scaled first, suppressing equally valid alternatives. RSVP is explicitly designed to resist such ratchets by enabling polycomputation: multiple encodings (field, token, associative, functional) scaling together, rather than one monopolizing all resources.
In cosmology, this means entropic backreaction alongside ΛCDM-like averages.
In computation, this means associative, neuromorphic, and dataflow components running in concert with sequential processors.
In culture, this means volumetric cinema alongside frame reels, or local actor substitutions alongside global blockbusters.
Scaling is not the enemy; it is the multiplier. But only if we keep the encodings plural.

\chapter{Introduction: Bitter Lessons, Sweet Lessons}
The narrative of progress in cinema, computation, and cosmology often appears linear, yet it is shaped by encoding ratchets—representational choices that, once standardized, scale efficiently and marginalize alternatives. Richard Sutton's Bitter Lesson (2019) posits that AI advances stem from computational scale over human-designed features \citep{Sutton2019}. Critics like Rodney Brooks advocate hybrid approaches, balancing design and scale \citep{Brooks2019}. In cosmology, the $\Lambda$CDM model's perturbative simplicity scales efficiently but may overlook non-linear backreaction effects \citep{Buchert2000}.
The Bitter Lesson teaches that methods which leverage computation to search large spaces tend to outperform handcrafted domain-specific knowledge in the long run. In practice, this has led to the dominance of deep learning over symbolic AI in tasks like image recognition and natural language processing. However, the "sweet lesson" counterpoint suggests that intelligent design can accelerate progress when combined with scaling. For instance, transformer architectures represent a designed innovation that unlocked new scaling regimes in language models.
Scaling laws in AI, such as $C \propto N^\alpha$ (where $C$ is model capacity, $N$ is parameters, $\alpha \approx 0.5$) \citep{Kaplan2020}, mirror cosmological scaling, e.g., void abundance $n_v \propto a^{-3(1+w_v)}$, $w_v \approx -1/3$ \citep{Giani2025}. Derivation: for voids, number density evolves with scale factor $a$ via the effective equation of state $w_v$, reflecting entropic expansion dynamics. This analogy highlights how early representational choices in cosmology, like assuming homogeneity, constrain what scales effectively.
Historical context reveals that the Bitter Lesson echoes Moore's Law debates, where hardware scaling drove computational paradigms \citep{Ceruzzi2003}. In cosmology, Einstein's static universe yielded to expanding models post-Hubble \citep{Hubble1929}, but not without resistance due to entrenched encodings of stability.
Worked example: For a neural network with $N=10^6$ parameters at $T=300$ K, entropy production per training step, via Landauer's principle ($E_{\min} = k_B T \ln 2$), is $S \approx k_B \ln 2 \times 10^6$ \citep{Landauer1961}. This mirrors cosmological entropy in structure formation, where void expansion dissipates information similarly to parameter updates pruning neural connections.
RSVP advocates pluralistic encodings to resist scaling-induced monocultures, positioning itself as a meta-framework that allows for both bitter scaling and sweet design in cosmological modeling.

In RSVP (Relativistic Scalar-Vector Plenum), these orders correspond to successive bifurcations in the scalar-vector-entropy field dynamics.
