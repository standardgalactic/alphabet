Who coined the word endosymbiosis?
Editar
The term "endosymbiosis" was coined by
Lynn Margulis
(formerly Lynn Sagan) in the 1960s. She introduced this term as part of her groundbreaking work on what became known as endosymbiotic theory, which explains how eukaryotic cells evolved through the incorporation of bacterial endosymbionts.
Margulis first presented her ideas in a 1967 paper titled "On the origin of mitosing cells" published in the Journal of Theoretical Biology. In this work, she proposed that organelles like mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria that became permanent residents inside host cells through endosymbiosis.
While the concept of organisms living inside other organisms had been observed before, Margulis was the one who formalized the terminology and developed the comprehensive theory explaining how this process led to the evolution of complex eukaryotic cells. Her ideas were initially met with skepticism but are now widely accepted as a fundamental principle in evolutionary biology.

Abstract
An enigmatic stepwise increase in oxygen in the late Precambrian is widely considered a prerequisite for the expansion of animal life. Accumulation of oxygen requires organic matter burial in sediments, which is largely controlled by the sheltering or preservational effects of detrital clay minerals in modern marine continental margin depocenters. Here, we show mineralogical and geochemical evidence for an increase in clay mineral deposition in the Neoproterozoic that immediately predated the first metazoans. Today most clay minerals originate in biologically active soils, so initial expansion of a primitive land biota would greatly enhance production of pedogenic clay minerals (the "clay mineral factory"), leading to increased marine burial of organic carbon via mineral surface preservation.
Editar
Thank you for sharing that! This is a fascinating 2006 Science paper by Kennedy, Droser, and colleagues that presents a really interesting hypothesis about late Precambrian oxygenation.
The key argument they're making is that there's a connection between:
The "clay mineral factory"
- referring to enhanced clay mineral production, likely from the expansion of primitive land-based life (early terrestrial biota) that would have accelerated soil formation and weathering processes
Increased organic carbon burial
- clay minerals are excellent at preserving organic matter in marine sediments by providing protective surfaces and creating anoxic microenvironments
Atmospheric oxygenation
- when more organic carbon gets buried in sediments (rather than being oxidized), it allows oxygen to accumulate in the atmosphere
The timing connection they identify is crucial - they show evidence that increased clay mineral deposition in Neoproterozoic sediments occurred just before the appearance of the first metazoans (early animals). This supports the idea that enhanced clay mineral production helped drive the oxygenation that was necessary for complex animal life.
It's a clever hypothesis because it links terrestrial biological processes (early land life creating soils) to marine geochemical processes (organic matter preservation) to atmospheric evolution (oxygen accumulation) - showing how the evolution of life on land may have helped create conditions for complex life in the oceans.
