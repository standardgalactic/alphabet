Key Threads
David Starr Jordan's Life
Jordan (1851-1931) was a pioneering ichthyologist (fish scientist) and Stanford's first president.
He dedicated his life to classifying fish, believing naming brought order to chaos.
A catastrophic earthquake in 1906 destroyed his specimen collection, but he obsessively rebuilt it.
Later, his darker side emerges: he became a leading figure in the American
eugenics movement
.
The Title Claim: "Fish Don't Exist"
Taxonomically, "fish" isn't a valid category.
What we casually call "fish" are spread across multiple branches of the evolutionary tree—some more closely related to land animals than to each other.
So, the concept of "fish" as a unified, scientific group collapses.
Author's Personal Story
Lulu Miller weaves in her own struggles: with depression, relationships, and questions of meaning.
She admires Jordan's resilience at first, but later confronts his dangerous faith in imposing order (eugenics).
Through this, she reflects on her own search for stability in a chaotic world.
Themes & Insights
Chaos vs. order:
Attempts to neatly categorize life can obscure reality.
Resilience:
Rebuilding after loss can be inspiring, but also blind.
Ethics of science:
Scientific classification is never neutral; it can be weaponized.
Identity & humility:
Accepting that categories (like "fish") are unstable frees us to see the living world—and ourselves—more truthfully.
Takeaway
The book isn't just about taxonomy. It's a meditation on how humans face
chaos
: some try to control it with rigid systems, while others learn to live within it. Miller concludes that embracing uncertainty may be healthier and more humane than clinging to false order.
