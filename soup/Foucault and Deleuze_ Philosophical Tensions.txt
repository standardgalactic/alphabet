Wendy Grace holds an Honorary Research position at both the University of Western Australia and the University of Queensland. She is the author, with Alec McHoul of A Foucault Primer (1992)
https://amzn.to/45RfGOI
. Wendy has taught on Foucault and 20th century French intellectual history at UWA, as well as the history of anthropological ideas at Murdoch University. Her research interests include 18th and 19th century French and German intellectual history. Her current research relates to Foucault's account of the Malthusian Couple within the history of heterosexuality, with particular focus on the scientific uncoupling of pleasure and procreation in the 19th century. France occupies a singular position in debates about Nietzsche, and Foucault and Deleuze are invariably singled out as French Nietzscheans par excellence. But what does this label "Nietzschean" really mean? Is it useful or misleading for understanding the respective trajectories of Foucault and Deleuze, not to mention the nebulous umbrella term "post-structuralism"? Many commentators have assumed that Foucault and Deleuze were propelled by the same Nietzsche, a man who lived during the 1870s and 1880s as a "philosopher." I argue that this locks Nietzsche into the history of philosophy, overlooking his role inaugurating a history of Western culture, otherwise known in Foucauldian terms as the history of systems of thought. As Foucault argued during the Colloque de Royaumont in July 1964, "The history of philosophy should not be confused with an archaeology of thought." Moreover, the philosophical understanding more readily suits Deleuze's appropriation of Nietzsche as philosophe maudit - even granting the difficulty of pinning down a Nietzschean system in the first place. But while Deleuze reads Nietzsche as a "counter philosopher", Foucault admires and emulates Nietzsche in a role I would call "ethnographer of the present." Of all concepts associated with post-structuralism, "difference" has curiously evaded critical scrutiny. But difference has opposite if not contradictory meanings in Deleuze and Foucault. Essentially for Deleuze, difference is internal to the individual, immediate (non-representational), and elucidated through a strictly philosophical method. For Foucault, on the other hand, difference is external, dependent on representational truth regimes for its effects, and made manifest through various interpretative strategies broadly ethnographic and comparative.

Internal to the individual
Immediate and non-representational
Understood through philosophical methods

Dependent on representational truth regimes

This section of Grace's transcript provides rich historical detail about the collaboration between Foucault and Deleuze before their 1976 split. Several important themes emerge:
The Denazification of Nietzsche
: Grace explains how French intellectuals systematically rehabilitated Nietzsche after WWII, with Bataille declaring that "fascism and Nietzschism are mutually and violently excluded from one another." The 1964 Royaumont conference symbolized this rehabilitation, where both Foucault and Deleuze presented papers on Nietzsche's most problematic concepts (will to power and eternal return).
Collaborative Scholarly Work
: Foucault and Deleuze worked together on the authoritative Colli-Montinari edition of Nietzsche's works, contributing to the general preface of the French translation. This shows their shared commitment to rigorous Nietzsche scholarship.
The Specific Intellectual
: After May 1968, they developed the concept of the "specific intellectual" as opposed to Sartre's "universal intellectual." Their key insight was what Deleuze called "the indignity of speaking for others" - intellectuals should speak from their specific expertise rather than claiming universal authority.
Prison Activism
: Both joined the Groupe d'Information sur les Prisons (GIP), founded by Daniel Defert (Foucault's partner). This group emerged just before spontaneous prison riots erupted across France in 1971-72, giving their activism immediate relevance. Their approach embodied the "specific intellectual" model - they didn't claim to speak for prisoners but tried to amplify prisoners' own voices.
Anti-Psychoanalytic Alliance
: Grace details their shared opposition to psychoanalysis's dominance in French intellectual life:
Deleuze and Guattari's
Anti-Oedipus
was the first text to seriously shake the psychoanalytic establishment
Foucault's
The History of Sexuality
challenged psychoanalytic approaches to sexuality
Both saw psychoanalysis as a form of social control rather than liberation
Personal and Political Solidarity
: Grace includes touching details like Deleuze writing with Defert about homosexual rights in prison, showing how their collaboration extended beyond abstract theory to concrete political issues.
The richness of this collaboration makes their 1976 split all the more dramatic - they went from being close intellectual and political allies to never speaking again. This sets up the significance of whatever happened with Deleuze's critique of Foucault's work on sexuality.

This section reveals the heart of Grace's argument about the Foucault-Deleuze split. She systematically dismantles previous explanations and offers her own compelling interpretation:
Dismissing Previous Explanations
: Grace reviews various biographers' attempts to explain the rift:
Eribon: No actual rupture, just stopped seeing each other / political considerations
Miller: Multiple reasons (though Deleuze was vague in his response)
Macey: Political strain over Jewish-Palestinian politics and German left-wing groups
Dosse: Implies Deleuze was jealous of Foucault's fame, plus political disagreements
Grace's Core Argument
: The split was fundamentally about
sexuality
- not just as a topic, but as lived experience and theoretical framework. She argues that Foucault's homosexuality gave him a radically different perspective on sexuality's political dimensions than the heterosexual Deleuze.
Sexuality as Central to Foucault's Work
: Grace demonstrates that sexuality wasn't a later addition to Foucault's thinking but was present from his earliest work:
1961's
History of Madness
already used sexuality to illustrate his historical method
He traced how the classical period created new intolerance toward male homosexuality
The concept of "erudite libertinage" and lyrical homosexuality disappeared with the Renaissance
Intense same-sex friendships became suspicious in the 18th century
The Theoretical Disagreement
: Grace identifies the crucial passage from Deleuze's "Desire and Pleasure" that reveals their fundamental disagreement. Deleuze writes about desire involving "the constitution of a field of immanence or a body without organs defined solely by zones of intensity, limits, gradients, fluxes. This body is just as much biological as collective and political."
Why This Made Foucault's "Flesh Crawl"
: Grace argues this biological language was antithetical to everything Foucault stood for. Foucault's entire project challenged:
Biological conceptions of subjectivity
The scientificization of human experience
Natural or essential explanations of sexuality
This represents a fundamental methodological and political disagreement: Deleuze's philosophy retained biological/naturalistic elements that Foucault saw as precisely the problem to be overcome. For Foucault, sexuality was entirely a historical construction; for Deleuze, it retained some biological/natural dimension.
Grace's interpretation is compelling because it connects their personal differences (gay vs. straight) to deep theoretical disagreements about the nature of sexuality, biology, and power.
