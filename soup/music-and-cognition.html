A summary of Music as Embodied cognition by Arnie Cox

Arnie Cox’s “Music as Embodied Cognition” explores the idea that our experience and understanding of music are deeply rooted in bodily engagement. This theory is centered around the concept of embodied imitation, which suggests that when we listen to music, we internally mimic the actions required to produce the sounds we hear.

  1. Mimetic Hypothesis: Cox’s mimetic hypothesis posits that listeners simulate the physical movements and exertions involved in creating music, whether consciously or unconsciously. This process isn’t just passive listening but an active, embodied participation that can blur the lines between performer and listener.

  2. Mimetic Proxy Theory: Building on this idea, mimetic proxy theory argues that our understanding of others’ actions—including musical performance—relies on using our own bodies as “proxies.” Listeners can empathize with musicians by internally simulating their actions, allowing for a shared, distributed understanding.

  3. Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) and “Mind Meld” Metaphor: Cox’s framework draws parallels between embodied musical engagement and horizontal gene transfer in biology. Just as HGT allows genetic material to move laterally between organisms, bypassing traditional inheritance, embodied imitation enables the rapid, non-linear sharing of musical ideas, emotions, and expressive states. This musical “horizontal transfer” resembles a form of “mind melding,” where shared bodily experiences create a collective understanding across individuals.

In summary, Cox’s theory illustrates how music functions as a medium for interpersonal connection, akin to “mind melding.” Through embodied imitation, neural resonance, and shared bodily experience, musical ideas, emotions, and meanings can be rapidly and deeply transmitted between individuals, fostering a collective musical consciousness.
